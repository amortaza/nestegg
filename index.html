    How tax is calculated

    Each income source is taxed at its own flat rate. Every month, its gross amount is reduced by that percentage:

    after-tax = gross × (1 − tax rate)

    • Regular income: the gross is the monthly amount including its yearly growth, so the tax grows with it.
    • Investments: only the monthly withdrawal is taxed. Growth inside the balance and the remaining balance are not taxed.
    • Expenses are never taxed.

    There are no brackets, deductions, or combining of sources. To approximate a progressive tax, enter your effective (average) rate.

    Stock (brokerage account)

    A regular taxable account. It is not a retirement account: there are no contribution limits, no age rules and no required withdrawals.

    Contributions

    • Any amount, any time, with money you've already paid income tax on. Nothing is deductible.

    Tax treatment

    • Cost basis: generally what you paid for an investment, including commissions. Gains and losses are measured against it.
    • Realized gains: growth isn't taxed until you sell. Held more than 1 year: long-term, taxed at 0%, 15% or 20% depending on taxable income. Held 1 year or less: short-term, taxed as ordinary income.
    • Dividends: taxed in the year paid, even if reinvested. “Qualified” dividends get long-term capital gains rates; other dividends are ordinary income.
    • Interest (for example from bonds or cash in the account): generally taxable in the year it's paid or credited.
    • Losses: realized losses offset gains. If losses are bigger, up to $3,000 a year ($1,500 married filing separately) can offset other income (tax year 2026); the rest carries forward.
    • Higher earners may also owe the net investment income tax.

    Withdrawals

    • Sell or withdraw any time. There's no early-withdrawal tax and no required minimum distribution; tax depends only on gains, dividends and interest.

    In this app

    • The capital gains tax rate is applied only to growth above what you put in (initial amount plus contributions) to show the “after tax” value at the end date.
    • Growth is one steady yearly rate. Yearly tax on dividends and interest, short- vs long-term holding periods, and losses aren't modeled.
    • The payout income created from this account taxes each whole withdrawal at that rate; it doesn't exclude your basis, so it overstates tax on withdrawals.

    IRS sources: Topic 409, Capital gains and losses · Topic 404, Dividends · Topic 403, Interest received · Topic 703, Basis of assets

    401(k)

    An employer-sponsored retirement plan. Your plan's own rules can be stricter than the IRS rules below—check your plan documents.

    Contributions

    • Your limit (pre-tax and Roth deferrals combined, across all your 401(k) and 403(b) plans): $24,500 for 2026; $25,500 for 2027.
    • Catch-up, if you're 50 or older by year-end and the plan allows it: an extra $8,000 for 2026 ($8,500 for 2027). If you turn 60–63 during the year, the catch-up is $11,250 instead for 2026 ($11,750 for 2027).
    • Employee vs. combined limit: the limit above covers only your own deferrals. A separate cap covers everything added to your account by one employer—your deferrals plus matching and other employer contributions: the lesser of 100% of your pay or $72,000 for 2026 (catch-ups are on top).
    • Employer match: optional and set by your plan. It usually depends on how much of your pay you contribute and is often capped at a percentage of pay; only pay up to $360,000 counts (2026). Matches may vest over several years—unvested amounts can be forfeited if you leave.

    Tax treatment

    • Traditional (pre-tax): deferrals aren't taxed as pay now; contributions and earnings are taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn. Employer matches are typically pre-tax too.
    • Roth deferrals (a “designated Roth account,” if your plan offers one): taxed as pay now. A qualified withdrawal is tax-free, earnings included. Qualified means after a 5-tax-year period that starts January 1 of the year of your first Roth deferral to that plan, and at age 59½, disability or death. A non-qualified withdrawal is partly taxable (the earnings share). There's no income limit for Roth deferrals.

    Withdrawals

    • Generally allowed only after you leave the employer, reach 59½, become disabled, die, the plan ends, or for a hardship your plan allows. Pre-tax amounts are taxed as ordinary income.

    Early withdrawals (before 59½)

    • General rule: a 10% additional tax on the taxable part, on top of income tax.
    • Common exceptions: leaving that job in or after the year you turn 55 (50 for qualifying public-safety workers)—this applies to that employer's plan, not IRAs; disability; death; substantially equal periodic payments; medical costs above 7.5% of AGI; payments to an alternate payee under a QDRO (a divorce or support order); a birth or adoption; one emergency withdrawal a year; terminal illness; domestic-abuse victims; qualified disasters.

    Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

    • Start: by April 1 of the year after you reach your RMD age, then by December 31 each year. A missed RMD can cost a 25% excise tax (10% if corrected in time).
    • RMD age by birth date: before July 1, 1949: 70½. July 1, 1949–1950: 72. 1951–1959: 73. 1960 or later: 75 under the SECURE 2.0 Act—the IRS pages reviewed here still describe only age 73, so confirm on IRS.gov.
    • Still working: if your plan allows and you own 5% or less of the employer, RMDs from that employer's plan can wait until April 1 of the year after you retire.
    • Roth deferrals: no RMDs while you're alive.

    In this app

    • Contribution and match are fixed monthly amounts with steady growth. Deferral, catch-up and combined limits, match caps and vesting aren't applied or checked.
    • The mirrored expense is your contribution only; the employer match isn't counted as your expense.
    • Tax on withdrawal is applied to the whole ending balance for the “after tax” value and to each payout. For Roth deferrals, set it to 0%—the app then treats every payout as qualified.
    • The early-withdrawal tax, its exceptions, and RMDs aren't modeled. The payout uses the withdrawal % you choose, which may be less than an RMD.

    IRS sources: 401(k) contribution limits · 2027 limits (IR-2026-136) · 401(k) distribution rules · Designated Roth accounts · Early-distribution exceptions · RMDs

    Roth IRA

    An individual retirement account funded with money you've already paid tax on.

    Contributions

    • Shared IRA limit: your total for all traditional and Roth IRAs is $7,500 ($8,600 if 50 or older) for both 2026 and 2027—or your taxable compensation, if less. A non-working spouse filing jointly can contribute based on the couple's pay.
    • Income eligibility (modified AGI): the amount you can contribute phases out between $153,000–$168,000 single or head of household and $242,000–$252,000 married filing jointly for 2026; $158,000–$173,000 and $250,000–$260,000 for 2027. Married filing separately: $0–$10,000 (not adjusted yearly).
    • Not deductible. Conversions (moving pre-tax money in from a traditional IRA or plan) are a separate step: the converted pre-tax amount is taxed that year.

    Withdrawals and tax treatment

    Money comes out in this order, across all your Roth IRAs:

    • 1. Regular contributions — tax- and penalty-free at any time.
    • 2. Conversions (oldest first) — no income tax again, but each conversion has its own 5-year clock: taking the taxable converted amount out within 5 years while under 59½ can trigger the 10% additional tax unless an exception applies.
    • 3. Earnings — tax-free only in a qualified distribution; otherwise taxable, and before 59½ generally subject to the 10% additional tax.

    Qualified distribution: made after a 5-tax-year period that begins January 1 of the first tax year for which you contributed to any Roth IRA—not when the account was opened (a 2025 contribution made in April 2026 starts the clock on January 1, 2025)—and at age 59½ or older, because of disability, to a beneficiary after death, or for a first home (up to $10,000 lifetime).

    Early withdrawals (before 59½)

    • Only earnings and recent conversions are exposed to the 10% additional tax. Exceptions include disability, death, substantially equal periodic payments, medical costs above 7.5% of AGI, health insurance while unemployed, higher-education costs, a first home, a birth or adoption, one emergency withdrawal a year, terminal illness, domestic-abuse victims and qualified disasters.

    Required minimum distributions

    • None for the original owner while alive. Beneficiaries who inherit a Roth IRA do have to take distributions.

    In this app

    • There's no tax setting: the balance and every payout are treated as tax-free, as if every withdrawal were qualified.
    • It doesn't track contributions vs. conversions vs. earnings, the 5-year rules, age 59½, income eligibility, or contribution limits.

    IRS sources: IRA contribution limits · 2026 limits (IR-2025-111) · 2027 limits (IR-2026-136) · Pub. 590-B, IRA distributions

    Traditional IRA

    An individual retirement account where contributions may be tax-deductible and growth is tax-deferred.

    Contributions

    • Who can contribute: anyone with taxable compensation, at any age (a non-working spouse filing jointly can use the couple's pay).
    • Shared IRA limit: your total for all traditional and Roth IRAs is $7,500 ($8,600 if 50 or older) for both 2026 and 2027—or your taxable compensation, if less.

    Tax treatment

    • Deduction: fully deductible if neither you nor your spouse is covered by a retirement plan at work. If one of you is, it phases out by modified AGI and filing status:
      • Single, covered at work: $81,000–$91,000 (2026); $83,000–$93,000 (2027).
      • Married filing jointly, you're covered: $129,000–$149,000 (2026); $133,000–$153,000 (2027).
      • You're not covered but your spouse is: $242,000–$252,000 (2026); $250,000–$260,000 (2027).
      • Married filing separately, covered: $0–$10,000 (not adjusted yearly).
    • Nondeductible contributions create basis (tracked on Form 8606). Withdrawals are then only partly taxable: each one is split pro-rata between basis and pre-tax money across all your traditional IRAs.
    • Deductible contributions and all earnings are taxed as ordinary income when withdrawn.

    Withdrawals

    • Allowed any time; the taxable part is ordinary income.

    Early withdrawals (before 59½)

    • General rule: a 10% additional tax on the taxable part.
    • Common exceptions: disability, death, substantially equal periodic payments, medical costs above 7.5% of AGI, health insurance while unemployed, higher-education costs, a first home (up to $10,000 lifetime), a birth or adoption, one emergency withdrawal a year, terminal illness, domestic-abuse victims, qualified disasters. The age-55 “left my job” exception does not apply to IRAs.

    Required minimum distributions (RMDs)

    • Start: by April 1 of the year after you reach your RMD age, then by December 31 each year—even if you're still working (there's no still-working exception for IRAs). A missed RMD can cost a 25% excise tax (10% if corrected in time).
    • RMD age by birth date: before July 1, 1949: 70½. July 1, 1949–1950: 72. 1951–1959: 73. 1960 or later: 75 under the SECURE 2.0 Act—the IRS pages reviewed here still describe only age 73, so confirm on IRS.gov.

    In this app

    • Tax on withdrawal is applied to the whole ending balance and to each payout. Deductions, basis and pro-rata withdrawals aren't modeled—if you have basis, enter an effective rate.
    • Contribution limits, eligibility, the early-withdrawal tax and RMDs aren't applied. The payout uses the withdrawal % you choose, which may be less than an RMD.

    IRS sources: IRA contribution limits · 2026 limits (IR-2025-111) · 2027 limits (IR-2026-136) · Pub. 590-B, IRA distributions · RMDs
