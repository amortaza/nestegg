    How tax is calculated

    Each income source is taxed at its own flat rate. Every month, its gross amount is reduced by that percentage:

    after-tax = gross × (1 − tax rate)

    • Regular income: the gross is the monthly amount including its yearly growth, so the tax grows with it.
    • Investments: only the monthly withdrawal is taxed. Growth inside the balance and the remaining balance are not taxed.
    • Expenses are never taxed.

    There are no brackets, deductions, or combining of sources. To approximate a progressive tax, enter your effective (average) rate.

    The chart, net, piggy bank, and each source's total use after-tax amounts. Hover the summary chart to see the year's total taxes.

    Stock (brokerage account)

    A regular, taxable investment account with no special tax treatment.

    • Contributions: any amount, any time, from money you've already paid tax on.
    • Withdrawals: any time, with no penalties.
    • Taxes: dividends are taxed each year, and selling at a gain is taxed. Holdings kept over a year get long-term capital gains rates (0%, 15% or 20% federal, depending on income); shorter holdings are taxed as ordinary income.

    Here: the capital gains tax applies only to growth above what you put in, giving the after-tax value at the end date.

    401(k)

    An employer-sponsored retirement plan funded from your paycheck.

    • Contributions: usually pre-tax, lowering your taxable income now. Many employers match part of what you put in. IRS annual limits apply, with extra catch-up room from age 50.
    • Withdrawals: taxed as ordinary income. Before age 59½ there is generally a 10% penalty, with some exceptions (such as leaving your job in or after the year you turn 55).
    • Required minimum distributions generally start at age 73 (75 if born in 1960 or later).

    Here: the employer match adds that percentage of your monthly contribution, and the withdrawal tax gives the after-tax value at the end date. For a Roth 401(k), set the tax to 0%.

    Roth IRA

    An individual retirement account funded with money you've already paid tax on.

    • Contributions: limited each year by the IRS, and eligibility phases out at higher incomes. Your contributions (not earnings) can be taken out any time without tax or penalty.
    • Withdrawals: earnings come out tax- and penalty-free once you're 59½ and the account has been open at least 5 years.
    • No required minimum distributions during your lifetime.

    Here: there's no tax setting; the whole ending balance is yours.

    Traditional IRA

    An individual retirement account where contributions may be tax-deductible.

    • Contributions: limited each year by the IRS (one limit shared with any Roth IRA). The deduction phases out at higher incomes if you or your spouse have a workplace plan.
    • Withdrawals: taxed as ordinary income. Before age 59½ there is generally a 10% penalty, with some exceptions.
    • Required minimum distributions generally start at age 73 (75 if born in 1960 or later).

    Here: the withdrawal tax gives the after-tax value at the end date.

    General information, not tax advice. IRS rules and limits change every year.
